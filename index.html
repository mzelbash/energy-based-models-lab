Step 1 of 6

The idea: an energy is a score

An energy-based model gives every possible input one number. Low means it fits, high means it does not.

Key idea

Low energy means this is the kind of image the model has seen. High energy means it is not.

What to know

  • The model is one network that reads an input and returns one number, the energy E(x).
  • Low energy: looks like real data. High energy: does not, like random noise.
  • It does not pick one answer. It scores every answer, and choosing is a separate step.
  • Two forms: E(x) asks "is x plausible?", E(x, y) asks "do x and y go together?"
  • It is not a new architecture: any network that outputs one number can be an energy.

Step 2 of 6

From energy to probability

Physics gives one rule that turns any energy into a probability: the lower the energy, the more likely.

Key idea

The higher the energy, the rarer the state. The Boltzmann distribution makes that exact.

What to know

  • The rule: p(x) = e−E(x) / Z.
  • e−E turns energy into a weight: low energy, big weight, never negative.
  • Z adds up every weight so the probabilities sum to 1.
  • For images, Z must add up every possible image: about 101,888 of them for 28 × 28. Impossible.
  • The good news: training and sampling only need differences in energy, so Z cancels out.

The two problems this creates, and the two fixes

Problem

How do we make a low-energy image, not just score one?

Fix: Langevin sampling (step 4)
Problem

Z is impossible, so we cannot train by plain maximum likelihood.

Fix: contrastive divergence (step 5)
Takeaway: p(x) = e−E(x) / Z. Low energy, high probability. We never compute Z.

Step 3 of 6

The energy network E(x)

A small CNN that reads a 32 × 32 digit and returns one number. That number is the energy.

Key idea

The energy is just the output of a normal neural network. A trained one gives real digits lower energy than noise.

What to know

  • Data: MNIST digits, pixels scaled to [−1, 1], padded from 28 × 28 to 32 × 32.
  • Network: four stride-2 convolutions shrink the image, two dense layers end in one number.
  • The last layer is linear, so the energy can be any real number.
  • Swish instead of ReLU: it is smooth, and sampling needs smooth gradients with respect to the pixels.

Step 4 of 6

Langevin sampling: noise into a digit

The network only scores. To make an image, start from noise and walk downhill on the energy, one small step at a time.

Key idea

Freeze the network. Move the pixels a little downhill, add a little noise, repeat.

What to know

  • The update: x ← x − η ∇xE(x) + noise.
  • ∇xE says how each pixel should change to lower the energy.
  • The network weights are frozen. Only the image changes.
  • The noise keeps samples from getting stuck, so you get different digits.
  • Same network as step 3. Langevin is a procedure that uses it, not a second model.

Step 5 of 6

Training: real energy down, fake energy up

First the model makes fake images, then we train it to prefer the real ones. That is contrastive divergence.

Key idea

loss = E(real) − E(fake). The only way to make it smaller: real energy down, fake energy up.

What to know

  • Positives: real images. Negatives: what the current model makes with Langevin.
  • Negatives show the model where it is wrong right now: places it rates low that are not real.
  • Short runs are enough. Exact samples would cost thousands of steps per batch.
  • The replay buffer keeps old fakes, so their short runs add up over many batches.
  • No label tells the network which image is real. The minus sign in the loss does that job.

Step 6 of 6

Your classifier is secretly an EBM

The same 10 logits can be read two ways: "which digit?" and "is this a digit at all?"

Key idea

The energy of an image is minus the log of the softmax denominator. A normal classifier throws that number away.

What to know

  • The energy of a pair is just E(x, y) = −logity. High logit, low energy.
  • Softmax over those energies is the Boltzmann distribution over the 10 labels.
  • At test time this is just a normal classifier, read as energies. Same answer, same cost.
  • Softmax only sees which logit is biggest. The energy sees how big they are overall.
  • Normal training never teaches that overall level. JEM (Grathwohl et al., 2020) adds the energy loss from step 5 so it means something.

History and the full code

The classic restricted Boltzmann machine, and every piece of code behind this lab.

The restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM)

  • One of the earliest EBMs (Hinton and colleagues, 1980s). The deep EBM is its modern relative.
  • Visible units v: the pixels. Hidden units h: learned pattern detectors (strokes, curves).
  • Restricted: links only between the two layers, never inside a layer.
  • Energy: E(v, h) = −(vᵀWh + aᵀv + bᵀh). Low energy, likely.
  • Sampling: Gibbs, alternating v → h → v → h, one whole layer at a time.

Full Boltzmann machine

Links everywhere. Slow to sample.

Restricted (RBM)

Only visible to hidden. Fast to sample.
